\begin{abstract}

To study municipal wastewater treatment plants to deal with overflows and shocks better, instead of relying on deterministic models to simulate the working conditions of the plant, we could use the methods from this paper. We discuss in detail of the five-dimensional model proposed by Harris C J in 1977 for a two tank treatment plant, which includes a buffer tank to control flow better. After analysing the 3000 Monte-Carlo Simulations, we infer that the buffer tank absorbs about $15-20\%$ of the rough inflow variance, and with a $\approx2.5$ hour lag. Regardless, the plant breaches regulations in about a day of the inflow under heavy load (passive, that too), we propose a better system that has a feedback throttle mechanism that solves* it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}

\begin{itemize}

\item\textbf{The Engineering Problem}

Currently, to battle wastewater that major cities produce on a daily basis in huge amounts, we use bio-wastewater treatment plants, which use anaerobic digestion as its process to breakdown the filth into biodegradable matter, which is more safer than releasing the polluted water directly into natural water streams. The problems being neither the volume of the incoming water, nor the percentage of filth is constant, and unpredictable. Adding onto it, we will have sharp discharge peaks in the mornings and evenings, random flooding, and unaccounted industrial dumps of chemical waste every so often. We also have to account for delicate working conditions of the fellow bacterial slaves we have in custody.
		
		
	\begin{itemize}
		\item\textbf{Washout:} If the incoming flow is too erratic, the bacterial colony gets flushed out at a greater rate than their reproduction rate.
		
		\item\textbf{Toxic Shock:} An unaccounted spike in the toxic contaminants and pollutants that do pH alterations will cause the bio digestive system to fail and die faster.
		
	\end{itemize}

\item\textbf{Architecture}
\begin{itemize}
	\item\textbf{Tank 1}
	
	This tank is a buffer. Its purpose is to contain the surges, smooth out the flow, damping and mixing.
	
	\item\textbf{Tank 2}
	
	This is the fixed volume tank which contains the live bacteria. It receives the stream from tank 1 and does the bio breakdown.
\end{itemize}

%\break

\item \textbf{The need for Stochastic Calculus}

In general Chemical Engineering, these systems are modelled using ODEs which were derived from the equation of conservation of Mass:

\begin{equation*}
	\frac{d(\text{Mass})}{dt} = \text{MassIn} - \text{MassOut} - \text{MassUsed}
\end{equation*}

\smallskip

Bio-degradation relies on non-linear kinetics, like the Monod Equation:

\begin{equation*}
	\mu(S) = \mu_{\text{max}}\frac{S}{K_{S}+S}
\end{equation*}

\smallskip

This method assumes a smooth flow of the pollutant concentration. Since this function is concave ($\mu''(S)<0$), the predicted value will usually be "better" than the measured value, and will only provide a "Best Case" scenario, which is basically useless as it is very unlikely to happen.

\medskip

Additionally, deterministic models practically fails to answer questions such as:

\begin{itemize}
	\item How long the plant withstand an erratic inflow before it fails?
	\item What is the probability that the effluent pollution exceeds environmental safety limits during a 5-day cycle?
\end{itemize}


\end{itemize}

\section{Derivations and Formulations}


This widely used model invented by CJ Harris \cite{cjharris} , assumes the wastewater treatment plant as a 5-dimensional state vector: $$X_t=[X_t^1, X_t^2, X_t^3, X_t^4, X_t^5]^T$$

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
    \begin{tabular}{l p{4.5cm} l}
        \hline
        \textbf{\shortstack[l]{Variable}} & \textbf{Physical Meaning} & \textbf{Unit} \\
        \hline
        $X_t^1$ & Volume of fluid in Tank 1 & $\mathrm{m^3}$ \\
        \hline
        $X_t^2$ & The flow rate of entering fluid & $\mathrm{m^3/day}$ \\
        \hline
        $X_t^3$ & Pollutant concentration of the inflow & $\mathrm{mg/L}$ or $\mathrm{g/m^3}$ \\
        \hline
        $X_t^4$ & Pollutant concentration flowing from Tank 1 to Tank 2 & $\mathrm{mg/L}$ \\
        \hline
        $X_t^5$ & Pollutant concentration flowing out of Tank 2 & $\mathrm{mg/L}$ \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}



\subsection{\textbf{Equalization Tank Volume $X^1$}}

The mass balance of the wastewater tank is modeled from equation \ref{eq:mass_balance}:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mass_balance}
\frac{dV}{dt} = Q_{\text{out}} - Q_{\text{in}}
\end{equation}

We assume the best outflow rate from the tank 1 to be as such below:

\begin{equation*}
	Q^* = \frac{\alpha_1+1}{\beta_1}
\end{equation*}

where, $\beta _{1}$ is the feedback gain coefficient, and $\alpha _{1}$ is a scaling constant.

\smallskip

And the incoming flow is $X_t^2$, and if it exceeds this rate, the total derivative becomes  negative, which shows the accumulation of water in the tank.
\smallskip

So we have the equation,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Xt1}
	dX_t^1 = \left(\frac{\alpha_1 + 1}{\beta_1} - X_t^2\right)dt
\end{equation}


\medskip







\subsection{\textbf{Inflow Rate $X^2$}}

The inflow rate behaviour is determined by two major things: \textbf{Drift} (a flow trend), and \textbf{Diffusion} (environmental fluctuations or random noise).

The Drift term is just the constant growth with a negative feedback, given by:

\begin{equation*}
	\text{Drift Term}=\left(\alpha _{1}+1-\beta _{1}\frac{X_{t}^{2}}{X_{t}^{1}}\right)dt
\end{equation*}

\smallskip

When the inflow rate $X_t^2$ becomes too high with respect to the tank volume $X_t^1$, the feedback component ($-\beta_1 {X_t^2}/{X_t^1}$)  becomes larger than the first term, so it pulls back the flow rate towards balance.

\smallskip

We use the Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) or Feller square-root diffusion model \cite{wfeller} for random disturbances. This stochastic noise is from standard Brownian Motion $dW_t^1$, scaled with the state ratio, given by:

\begin{equation*}
	\text{Diffusion Term}=\sqrt{2\gamma _{1}\frac{X_{t}^{2}}{X_{t}^{1}}}dW_{t}^{1}
\end{equation*}

\smallskip

The boundary conditions at $X_t^2 \to 0$ when inflow stops\footnote{While the boundary condition of strict positive drift is a physical anomaly (since we literally cannot have zero inflow), it represents an immense pressure build-up somewhere upstream, and holds the non-negativity (for computation purposes) of the state variable during simulation.} are:

\begin{itemize}
	\item The diffusion coefficient becomes zero, so no random noise in the system.
	\item The drift remains strictly positive, $(\alpha_1 + 1)dt > 0$.
\end{itemize}

From this, we have:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Xt2}
	dX_{t}^{2}=\left(\alpha _{1}+1-\beta _{1}\frac{X_{t}^{2}}{X_{t}^{1}}\right)dt+\sqrt{2\gamma _{1}\frac{X_{t}^{2}}{X_{t}^{1}}}dW_{t}^{1}
\end{equation}







\medskip






\subsection{\textbf{Inflow Dirtiness $X^3$}}


The Dirtiness rate is determined by the following equation:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Xt3}
	dX_t^3 =
\frac{\alpha_2 + 1 - \beta_2 X_t^3}{X_t^1}\,dt
+
\sqrt{
2\gamma_2 \frac{X_t^3}{X_t^1}
}\,dW_t^2
\end{equation}

This equation shows the dirtiness of the incoming sewage $X_t^3$. It assumes the dirtiness tries to return to the normal average level always, and the water tank $X_t^1$ acts as a shield.

\smallskip

The first term of the equation tells us how the dirtiness gets pulled back to the baseline level when it gets very high or low, since:

\begin{equation*}
	\alpha_2 + 1 - \beta_2 X_t^3 = \beta_2\left(\frac{\alpha_2 + 1}{\beta_2} - X_t^3\right)
\end{equation*}

And this is with respect to the tank size ($X_t^1$), because a larger tank size will be able to slow down fast erratic changes better, and dilute the big spikes. 

The second part of the equation explains the randomness of the environmental noise. The square root is so that the dirt level is always possible, since it cant drop below zero. 






\medskip





\subsection{\textbf{Tank 1-2 Pollutant Concentration ($X^4$)}}


Currently, we have the total mass of the stuff inside the tank as $M_t = X_t^1 X_t^4$ (vol x concentration). That gives us,

\begin{align*}
	dM_t &= d(X^1 X^4)_t\\
	&= X_t^1\,dX_t^4 + X_t^4\,dX_t^1 + d\langle X^1, X^4 \rangle_t
\end{align*}

Change in mass is the difference between incoming and outgoing mass rate. So,

\begin{equation*}
	dM_t = \left( X_t^2 X_t^3 - X_{\text{out}} X_t^4 \right) dt
\end{equation*}

If we assume that the tank's liquid changes slowly, relative to the concentration changes, we can take $dX_t^1 \approx 0$, and $X_\text{out} \approx X_t^2$. And since $X^1$ has zero diffusion,  $d\langle X^1, X^4 \rangle_t = 0$. So substituting these will get us,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Xt4}
	dX_t^4 = (X_t^3 - X_t^4) \frac{X_t^2}{X_t^1}\,dt
\end{equation}


\smallskip

The difference term is that when the incoming sewage is dirtier than the current water, the concentration of the tank increases. If the opposite, the concentration drops. The ratio term is called the \textbf{Turnover Rate}. It is the inverse of hydraulic retention time. When this retention time is long, the ratio will become small, and the tank will act like a smoothen out volatile inflow into a stable stream, $X_4$.




\medskip







\subsection{\textbf{Treated Pollutant Concentration ($X^5$)}}

Conservation of mass gives us,

\begin{equation}
	\frac{dX^5}{dt} = \underbrace{\frac{Q}{V}(X^4 - X^5)}_{\text{Hydraulic dilution}} - \underbrace{R(X^5)}_{\text{Microbial consumption}}
\end{equation}

\smallskip

where V is the reactor volume and Q is the flow.

The Monod Equation for microbial consumption \cite{monodeq} is, 

\begin{equation*}
	R(X^5) = \frac{\mu_{\max} X_{\text{bacteria}}}{Y} \left( \frac{X^5}{K_s + X^5} \right)
\end{equation*}

Using Taylor series we can expand this into,

\begin{align*}
	R(X^5) &\approx R(X^*) + R'(X^*)(X^5 - X^*)\\
	&= \underbrace{( R(X^*) - R'(X^*)X^* )}_{-a} + {R'(X^*)} X^5\\
	&= -a + k X^5
\end{align*}


Let D be the diffusion rate, $D = Q/V$, and some $b \approx D + k$.

\begin{align*}
	\frac{dX^5}{dt} &= D(X^4 - X^5) - ( -a + k X^5 )\\
	&\approx a + b(X^4 - X^5)\\
\end{align*}

\smallskip

And finally,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Xt5}
	dX_t^5 = \left( a + b(X_t^4 - X_t^5) \right) dt
\end{equation}






\medskip








\subsection*{\textbf{Set of equations}}

\begin{align*}
	dX_t^1
&=
\left(
\frac{\alpha_1 + 1}{\beta_1} - X_t^2
\right)dt
\\[8pt]
dX_t^2
&=
\left(
\alpha_1 + 1
-
\beta_1 \frac{X_t^2}{X_t^1}
\right)dt
+
\sqrt{
2\gamma_1 \frac{X_t^2}{X_t^1}
}\,dW_t^1
\\[8pt]
dX_t^3
&=
\left(
\frac{\alpha_2 + 1 - \beta_2 X_t^3}{X_t^1}
\right)dt
+
\sqrt{
2\gamma_2 \frac{X_t^3}{X_t^1}
}\,dW_t^2
\\[8pt]
dX_t^4
&=
(X_t^3 - X_t^4)
\frac{X_t^2}{X_t^1}\,dt
\\[8pt]
dX_t^5
&=
\left(
a + b(X_t^4 - X_t^5)
\right)dt
\end{align*}





\medskip




\subsection*{\textbf{Matrix Notation}}


If we write the equations in the following form,

\begin{equation*}
	dX_t = f(X_t) dt + G(X_t) dW_t
\end{equation*}

we can write the set of equations as the sum of vectors in matrices form, such as:

\begin{equation*}
	\begin{bmatrix}
dX^1\\
dX^2\\
dX^3\\
dX^4\\
dX^5
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
f_1(X)\\
f_2(X)\\
f_3(X)\\
f_4(X)\\
f_5(X)
\end{bmatrix}dt
+
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0\\
\sqrt{2\gamma_1\frac{X^2}{X^1}} & 0\\
0 & \sqrt{2\gamma_2\frac{X^3}{X^1}}\\
0 & 0\\
0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
dW^1\\
dW^2
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation*}


\smallskip

We can see that only two out of the five equations have a diffusion term. So we can infer that the randomness exclusively enters through the inflow. The $X^4$ and $X^5$ become stochastic because they contain the terms with diffusion.

\smallskip

This system is called a \textbf{Hypoelliptic System}.







\bigskip








\section{Numerical Solving}

We use Euler-Maruyama method \cite{eulermaruyama} to solve these differential equations.

The interval $[0,T]$ is divided into $N$ equal steps of size,

\begin{equation*}
	\Delta t = \frac{T}{N}
\end{equation*}

and the discrete time being, $t_n = n \Delta t$.

\smallskip

Since Brownian Motion increments follow a normal distribution with mean $0$ and variance $\Delta t$, 

\begin{align*}
	\Delta W &= W_{t + \Delta t} - W_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Delta t)\\
	\Rightarrow \qquad \Delta W_n^k &= W_{(n+1)\Delta t}^k-W_{n\Delta t}^k\\
	&= \sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_n^k
\end{align*}

where $Z_n^k \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$



\medskip


\subsection*{\textbf{Solving the system of DE}}

When applying Euler-Maruyama method to the system, it gives:

\begin{equation}
	X_{n+1}^1 = X_n^1 + \left(  \frac{\alpha_1+1}{\beta_1} - X_n^2  \right) \Delta t
\end{equation}




\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    X_{n+1}^2
&=
X_n^2+
\left(\alpha_1+1-\beta_1\frac{X_n^2}{X_n^1}\right)\Delta t
\\
&\quad+
\sqrt{2\gamma_1\frac{\widetilde{X}_n^2}{\widetilde{X}_n^1}}
\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_n^1
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}




\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    X_{n+1}^3
&=
X_n^3+
\left(
\frac{\alpha_2+1-\beta_2X_n^3}{X_n^1}
\right)\Delta t
\\
&\quad+
\sqrt{
2\gamma_2\frac{\widetilde{X}_n^3}{\widetilde{X}_n^1}
}
\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_n^2
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}



\begin{align}
	X_{n+1}^4
&=
X_n^4+
(X_n^3-X_n^4)
\frac{X_n^2}{X_n^1}\Delta t
\end{align}




\begin{align}
	X_{n+1}^5
&=
X_n^5+
\left(
a+b(X_n^4-X_n^5)
\right)\Delta t
\end{align}


\smallskip


Since the $X^2$ and $X^3$ terms contain a random variable, if majority of their draws are negative, their values can go to negative. While solving, these negative values can cause imaginary values. So, we set hard boundaries to the values:

\begin{align*}
	\widetilde{X}_n^i&=\max(X_n^i,0)\\
\widetilde{X}_n^1&=\max(X_n^1,\epsilon)
\end{align*}

\smallskip

where $\epsilon = 10^{-4}$. This prevents zero in the denominator as well.







\bigskip








\section{Simulation and Data Analysis\label{section4}}

The initial values of the simulation are as follows:
\smallskip

\begin{itemize}
	\item Number of Monte-Carlo paths: $N_{\text{paths}} = 3000$
	\item Runtime: $T = 5$ days
	\item Step size: $\Delta t = 0.0025$ days
	\item Time steps: $2000$
\end{itemize}

\smallskip

and the initial parameters are set as,

\begin{align*}
&\alpha_1=1.0,
&&\beta_1=1.2,
&&\gamma_1=0.15,\\
&\alpha_2=1.0,
&&\beta_2=1.0,
&&\gamma_2=0.20,\\
&a=0.05,
&&b=1.50
\end{align*}


\smallskip


The initial state vector is:

\begin{equation*}
	X_0 = [10.0, \frac{\alpha_1 + 1}{\beta_1}, 5.0, 4.0, 1.0]^T
\end{equation*}


And $(\alpha_1 + 1)/\beta_1$ turned out to be $\approx1.667$ for the initial condition. Hence, we have

\begin{equation*}
X_0=[10.0,\;1.667,\;5.0,\;4.0,\;1.0]^T	
\end{equation*}





\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Euler-Maruyama Discretization Scheme}
\label{alg:euler_maruyama}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
X = np.zeros((5, N_steps + 1, N_paths))
X[:, 0, :] = np.array([X1_0, X2_0, 
                       X3_0, X4_0, 
                       X5_0])[:, None]

for n in range(N_steps):
    x1 = np.maximum(X[0, n, :], 1e-4)
    x2 = np.maximum(X[1, n, :], 0.0)
    x3 = np.maximum(X[2, n, :], 0.0)
    x4, x5 = X[3, n, :], X[4, n, :]

    Z1 = np.random.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=N_paths)
    Z2 = np.random.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=N_paths)
    diff2 = np.sqrt(2.0 * gamma1 * x2 / x1) * sq_dt * Z1
    diff3 = np.sqrt(2.0 * gamma2 * x3 / x1) * sq_dt * Z2

    X[0, n+1, :] = x1 + (((alpha1 + 1.0) / beta1) 
                         - x2) * dt
    X[1, n+1, :] = x2 + (alpha1 + 1.0 
                         - beta1 * (x2 / x1)) * dt + diff2
    X[2, n+1, :] = x3 + ((alpha2 + 1.0 
                         - beta2 * x3) / x1) * dt + diff3
    X[3, n+1, :] = x4 + ((x3 - x4) * (x2 / x1)) * dt
    X[4, n+1, :] = x5 + (a + b * (x4 - x5)) * dt
\end{lstlisting}
\end{algorithm}

\smallskip


From Figure \ref{fig:reduction_ratio}, we could notice that the ratio $\mathbb{E}[X_t^5/X_t^3]$ goes above 1 at around day 2, which would mean that the plant is generating more pollution than it takes, and it could be explained by the following:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Water Lag:} The treatment plant contains a water lag, so at a specific point in time, the outflowing water is not from the same inflow at the same time. The polluted water stays in the system for hours, or even days.
	\item \textbf{Jensen's Inequality:} 
		\begin{equation*}
			   \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{X^5}{X^3}\right]\neq\frac{\mathbb{E}[X^5]}{\mathbb{E}[X^3]}
		\end{equation*}
		
		\smallskip
		
		If we take just $X^3$, the function is $f(x) = 1/x$, which is convex for $x>0$, and so, when the inflow concentration is near zero, the ratio shoots up that skews the sample mean.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\textbf{Cumulative Mass Removal Efficiency ($\eta_{\text{mass}}(t)$)}}

The formula is given by,

\begin{equation}
	\eta_{\text{mass}}(t)
=
1-
\frac{
\int_0^t X_s^5X_s^2\,ds
}{
\int_0^t X_s^3X_s^2\,ds
}
\end{equation}




Figure \ref{fig:cumulative_mass_removal_efficiency} shows the cumulative mass removal starts at around 80\%, then gradually drops to around 6-8\% as the plant stabilises. The ratio is much smaller than expected, but this only tracks the plant's efficiency in such chaotic conditions. So this is pretty much accurate with physical systems as a worst case scenario.

\subsection{\textbf{Ratio of Expectations}}

The ratio is given by:

\begin{equation}
	\bar{R}(t)
=
\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_t^5]}
{\mathbb{E}[X_t^3]}
\end{equation}


Figure \ref{fig:macro_concentration_drop_ratio} shows that the ratio increases smoothly from $0.2$ to around $1.3$ during the low-inflow time. 





\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Cumulative Mass Removal and Macro Ratio}
\label{alg:mass_metrics}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
mass_flow_in = X[1, :, :] * X[2, :, :]
mass_flow_out = X[1, :, :] * X[4, :, :]

cum_mass_in = cumulative_trapezoid(
    mass_flow_in, t, axis=0, initial=0.0
)
cum_mass_out = cumulative_trapezoid(
    mass_flow_out, t, axis=0, initial=0.0
)

with np.errstate(divide="ignore", invalid="ignore"):
    removal_eff = (1.0 - (cum_mass_out 
                          / cum_mass_in)) * 100.0

removal_eff[0, :] = (1.0 - (X[4, 0, :] 
                            / X[2, 0, :])) * 100.0
mean_eff = np.mean(removal_eff, axis=1)

ratio_of_expectations = (
    np.mean(X[4, :, :], axis=1) 
    / np.maximum(np.mean(X[2, :, :], axis=1), 1e-4)
)
\end{lstlisting}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection*{\textbf{Variance Transmission Ratio ($\rho_{\text{damp}}(t)$)}}


The variance ratio between tank 1 treated water and raw wastewater:

\begin{equation*}
	\rho_{\text{damp}}(t)
=
\frac{\operatorname{Var}(X_t^4)}
{\operatorname{Var}(X_t^3)}
\end{equation*}



From figure \ref{fig:variance_damping_ratio}, we can see that the ratio starts from zero and climbs to around 0.85 or 85\% as the variance accumulates. This shows that the Tank 1 absorbs around 15-20\% of the raw variance under constant noise.





\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Variance Damping and Lag}
\label{alg:variance_damping}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
var_x3 = np.var(X[2, :, :], axis=1)
var_x4 = np.var(X[3, :, :], axis=1)
damping_ratio = var_x4 / np.maximum(var_x3, 1e-6)

x3_c = X[2, :, :] - np.mean(X[2, :, :], 
                            axis=0, keepdims=True)
x4_c = X[3, :, :] - np.mean(X[3, :, :], 
                            axis=0, keepdims=True)

max_lag = int(N_steps / 4)
lags = np.arange(-max_lag, max_lag + 1)
ccorr = np.zeros(len(lags))

for idx, lag in enumerate(lags):
    if lag < 0:
        ccorr[idx] = np.mean(x3_c[-lag:, :] 
                             * x4_c[:lag, :])
    elif lag > 0:
        ccorr[idx] = np.mean(x3_c[:-lag, :] 
                             * x4_c[lag:, :])
    else:
        ccorr[idx] = np.mean(x3_c * x4_c)

ccorr /= (np.std(X[2, :, :]) * np.std(X[3, :, :]))
tau_star = (lags * dt)[np.argmax(ccorr)]
\end{lstlisting}
\end{algorithm}

\section{A Better Predictor Model}


In Section \ref{section4}, the model used by Harris does the buffering of noise reduction and smoothening the inflow perfectly, but fails at being efficient, since it is a open-loop model. The model fails under an inflow shock, as the passage between the two tanks is passive, and when the reactor washes out, it breaches the regulation limits. To battle this, we can have an active feedback control shielding to the model.






\smallskip

\subsection{\textbf{Mathematical Formulation}}
	
In the passive model, the mass exchange rate equation is,

\begin{equation*}
	dX_t^5 = \left(a + b(X_t^4 - X_t^5)\right) dt
\end{equation*}

Here, $b\approx Q/V + k$ is a constant dilution rate. But for our new active model, we can have a variable-speed 'valve' that regulates the flow from tank 1 to tank 2, with respect to the current reactor stress. It is regulated by a parameter, $u(X_t^5) \in [u_{\min}, 1.0]$:

\begin{equation}
	u(X_t^5) = u_{\min} + \frac{1 - u_{\min}}{1 + \exp\left(k_c (X_t^5 - C_{\text{target}})\right)}
\end{equation}

\smallskip


where $C_\text{target}$ is a value that is below the regulation limits ($C_\text{crit}=3.5$), hence $C_\text{target}=2.6$ mg/L. $k_c = 6.0$ is gain, and $u_{\text{min}} = 0.05$ is to maintain circulation to avoid stagnant water.

\smallskip

So the equation \ref{eq:Xt5} becomes,

\begin{equation}
	dX_t^5 = \left(a + b \cdot u(X_t^5)(X_t^4 - X_t^5)\right) dt
\end{equation}


\smallskip

When the plant is working properly, $X^5 << C_\text{target}$, so $u(X_t^5)\approx 1$. And when the plant is struggling and $X^5\rightarrow C_\text{target}$, the controller throttles the flow, and gives enough time for the bacteria in tank 2 to sufficiently digest the pollutants.


\medskip




\subsection{\textbf{Simulation and Analysis}}

We simulated the model with $N_\text{paths} = 2000$ and the same $5$ day period.






\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Active Retention Throttling Controller}
\label{alg:active_controller}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
for n in range(N_steps):
    Z1 = np.random.normal(0, 1, N_paths)
    Z2 = np.random.normal(0, 1, N_paths)

    x1_c, x2_c, x3_c, x4_c, x5_c = X_ctrl[:, n, :]
    x1_ct = np.maximum(x1_c, 1e-4)
    x2_ct = np.maximum(x2_c, 0.0)
    x3_ct = np.maximum(x3_c, 0.0)

    u = 0.05 + 0.95 / (1.0 + np.exp(k_c * (x5_c - C_target)))

    dX1_c = (((alpha1 + 1.0) / beta1) - x2_c) * dt
    dX2_c = ((alpha1 + 1.0 - beta1 * (x2_c / x1_ct)) * dt
             + np.sqrt(2 * gamma1 * x2_ct / x1_ct) * sq_dt * Z1)
    dX3_c = (((alpha2 + 1.0 - beta2 * x3_c) / x1_ct) * dt
             + np.sqrt(2 * gamma2 * x3_ct / x1_ct) * sq_dt * Z2)
    dX4_c = (x3_c - x4_c) * (x2_c / x1_ct) * dt
    dX5_c = (a + b * u * (x4_c - x5_c)) * dt

    X_ctrl[:, n+1, :] = [
        x1_c + dX1_c, x2_c + dX2_c, x3_c + dX3_c,
        x4_c + dX4_c, x5_c + dX5_c
    ]
\end{lstlisting}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Recent Developments}



The model from 1977 developed by C J Harris is a really good starting point in understanding how the tank works, and how it smoothens the flow, reduces the waste. but it is very outdated, at nearly 50 years old. Since then, there have been many improvements to this model, such as:


\smallskip



\subsection{\textbf{Adding Spikes to the Continuous Noise}}


\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Problem:} In the older model, the noise comes from Standard Brownian Motion, which is continuous and not very unpredictable, unlike in real world scenarios. We would certainly face some spikes or rough flows in real life from time to time.
	\item \textbf{Solution:} People now add spikes called \textbf{Poisson Jump Process} ($dN_t$) with the brownian motion, which creates a new diffusion model,

\begin{equation*}
	dX_t^3 = \underbrace{dt}_{\text{Normal Drift}} + \underbrace{dW_t}_{\text{Wiggling}} + \underbrace{dN_t}_{\text{Spikes}}
\end{equation*}


\smallskip


This works by having the $dN_t$ at zero at most of the times, and every once in a while, there will be an instant spike in the inflow. \cite{applebaum}

\end{itemize}


\smallskip


\subsection{\textbf{Giving Memory to Noise}} 

\begin{itemize}
	\item\textbf{Problem:} Currently the model assumes if we have a very big inflow in this exact second, the flow a while from now is totally independent of the flow now, which is not very realistic. In real conditions, when it rains heavily, it doesn't stop in just a minute, but carry on for hours.
	
	\item\textbf{Solution:} We use something called Coloured Noise, with a method like the Ornstein Uhlenbeck process\cite{ornstein}. This noise has momentum and retention, so once it has a spike, it stays for a realistic amount of time.
	
\end{itemize}



\smallskip




\subsection{\textbf{Realistic Biology}}


\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Problem:} In this model, we simplified the bacterial process into a straight line equation $a + b(X_t^4 - X_t^5)$, but in reality it doesn't work like that. There are different kinds of bacteria that turns different stuff into different other stuff, and we have to account for all that. (For example, if one group of this bacterial works faster than the others, there will be an acid build up and they all die.)

	\item \textbf{Solution:} A really nice solution to this problem is to use Anaerobic Digestion Model (ADM1)\cite{batstone2002}. The catch is that this is a massive 20+ equations model that predict bacteria processes to near perfection. To make it stochastic, randomness is applied to the growth rate and death rates of the bacteria.

\end{itemize}



\smallskip




\subsection{\textbf{Better Computation}}

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Problem:} In our code, we have square roots which needs a domain of positive real numbers, else it will crash. To get around it we simply set any negative inputs to zero ($\texttt{np.maximum(x, 0)}$).

	\item \textbf{Solution:} To avoid this artificial clipping, we could use Itô's lemma \cite{dereich} to change the variables to $Y_t = \sqrt{X_t}$. This way, it removes the square root terms completely.
	
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}




In this course project, we learned about how a bio-wastewater treatment plant under heavy loads and unpredictable flow rates, simulated using Harris's five-dimensional stochastic model

From the simulation, we were able to infer the following:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{The Buffer Tank holds up (mostly):} The unstable inflow is contained in tank 1, and it holds up pretty well, acting as a shock absorbant, and slowing down the flow, reducing the flow variance by about 15-20\%.
	\item \textbf{Tank Failure:} Even with this tank 1, the tank still fails to uphold the regulations, and fails miserably only after 1 day of this rugged flow. This happens for a lot of reasons, but the main one being the 'perfect' tank 1. It smoothens out the flow a lot, but the flow is still a lot higher, which leads to the absolute demise of our fellow bacterial soldiers.
	\item \textbf{Fixes:} We added a feedback throttle mechanism, which throttles the flow from the tank 1 to tank 2, which fixes this increased flow problem, and gives us a significantly better result, at an efficiency around 90\%, and survival rate from 0\% to 80\%. The catch is that it needs a significantly larger tank 1 to hold in the extra sewage until the previous batch is process and the inflow slows down. A better alternative, but a costlier one indeed.
\end{itemize}
