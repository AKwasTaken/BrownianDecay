\section{Motivation}

\begin{itemize}

\item\textbf{The Engineering Problem}

Currently, to battle wastewater that major cities produce on a daily basis in huge amounts, we use bio-wastewater treatment plants, which use anaerobic digestion as its process to breakdown the filth into biodegradable matter, which is more safer than releasing the polluted water directly into natural water streams. The problems being neither the volume of the incoming water, nor the percentage of filth is constant, and unpredictable. Adding onto it, we will have sharp discharge peaks in the mornings and evenings, random flooding, and unaccounted industrial dumps of chemical waste every so often. We also have to account for delicate working conditions of the fellow bacterial slaves we have in custody.
		
		
	\begin{itemize}
		\item\textbf{Washout:} If the incoming flow is too erratic, the bacterial colony gets flushed out at a greater rate than their reproduction rate.
		
		\item\textbf{Toxic Shock:} An unaccounted spike in the toxic contaminants and pollutants that do pH alterations will cause the bio digestive system to fail and die faster.
		
	\end{itemize}

%\break

\begin{figure}[htbp] 
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{wastetanks_flowchart.png} 
    \caption{Water Treatment Plant Flowchart.}
    \label{fig:waste-flowchart}
\end{figure}

\item\textbf{Architecture}
\begin{itemize}
	\item\textbf{Tank 1}
	
	This tank is a buffer. Its purpose is to contain the surges, smooth out the flow, damping and mixing.
	
	\item\textbf{Tank 2}
	
	This is the fixed volume tank which contains the live bacteria. It receives the stream from tank 1 and does the bio breakdown.
\end{itemize}

%\break

\item \textbf{The need for Stochastic Calculus}

In general Chemical Engineering, these systems are modelled using ODEs which were derived from the equation of conservation of Mass:

\begin{equation*}
	\frac{d(\text{Mass})}{dt} = \text{MassIn} - \text{MassOut} - \text{MassUsed}
\end{equation*}

\smallskip

Bio-degradation relies on non-linear kinetics, like the Monod Equation:

\begin{equation*}
	\mu(S) = \mu_{\text{max}}\frac{S}{K_{S}+S}
\end{equation*}

\smallskip

This system assumes smooth deterministic flow of the pollutant concentration. Since this function is concave ($\mu''(S)<0$), the predicted value will usually be "better" than the measured value, and will only provide a "Best Case" scenario, which is basically useless as it is very unlikely to happen.

\medskip

Additionally, deterministic models practically fails to answer questions such as:

\begin{itemize}
	\item How long the plant withstand an erratic inflow before it fails?
	\item What is the probability that the effluent pollution exceeds environmental safety limits during a 5-day cycle?
\end{itemize}


\end{itemize}

\bigskip










\section{Derivations and Formulations}


This widely used model invented by CJ Harris \cite{cjharris} , assumes the wastewater treatment plant as a 5-dimensional state vector: $$X_t=[X_t^1, X_t^2, X_t^3, X_t^4, X_t^5]^T$$

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
    \begin{tabular}{l p{4.5cm} l}
        \hline
        \textbf{\shortstack[l]{Variable}} & \textbf{Physical Meaning} & \textbf{Unit} \\
        \hline
        $X_t^1$ & Volume of fluid in Tank 1 & $\mathrm{m^3}$ \\
        \hline
        $X_t^2$ & The flow rate of entering fluid & $\mathrm{m^3/day}$ \\
        \hline
        $X_t^3$ & Pollutant concentration of the inflow & $\mathrm{mg/L}$ or $\mathrm{g/m^3}$ \\
        \hline
        $X_t^4$ & Pollutant concentration flowing from Tank 1 to Tank 2 & $\mathrm{mg/L}$ \\
        \hline
        $X_t^5$ & Pollutant concentration flowing out of Tank 1 & $\mathrm{mg/L}$ \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}



\subsection{\textbf{Equalization Tank Volume $X^1$}}

The mass balance of the wastewater tank is modeled from equation \ref{eq:mass_balance}:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mass_balance}
\frac{dV}{dt} = Q_{\text{out}} - Q_{\text{in}}
\end{equation}

We assume the best outflow rate from the tank 1 to be as such below:

\begin{equation*}
	Q^* = \frac{\alpha_1+1}{\beta_1}
\end{equation*}

where, $\beta _{1}$ is the feedback gain coefficient, and $\alpha _{1}$ is a scaling constant.

\smallskip

And the incoming flow is $X_t^2$, and if it exceeds this rate, the total derivative becomes  negative, which shows the accumulation of water in the tank.
\smallskip

So we have the equation,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Xt1}
	dX_t^1 = \left(\frac{\alpha_1 + 1}{\beta_1} - X_t^2\right)dt
\end{equation}


\medskip







\subsection{\textbf{Inflow Rate $X^2$}}

The inflow rate behaviour is determined by two major things: \textbf{Drift} (a flow trend), and \textbf{Diffusion} (environmental fluctuations or random noise).

The Drift term is just the constant growth with a negative feedback, given by:

\begin{equation*}
	\text{Drift Term}=\left(\alpha _{1}+1-\beta _{1}\frac{X_{t}^{2}}{X_{t}^{1}}\right)dt
\end{equation*}

\smallskip

When the inflow rate $X_t^2$ becomes too high with respect to the tank volume $X_t^1$, the feedback component ($-\beta_1 {X_t^2}/{X_t^1}$)  becomes larger than the first term, so it pulls back the flow rate towards balance.

\smallskip

We use the Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) or Feller square-root diffusion model \cite{wfeller} for random disturbances. This stochastic noise is from standard Brownian Motion $dW_t^1$, scaled with the state ratio, given by:

\begin{equation*}
	\text{Diffusion Term}=\sqrt{2\gamma _{1}\frac{X_{t}^{2}}{X_{t}^{1}}}dW_{t}^{1}
\end{equation*}

\smallskip

The boundary conditions at $X_t^2 \to 0$ when inflow stops\footnote{While the boundary condition of strict positive drift is a physical anomaly (since we literally cannot have zero inflow), it physically represents an immense pressure build-up somewhere upstream, and computationally ensures the non-negativity of the state variable during simulation.} are:

\begin{itemize}
	\item The diffusion coefficient becomes zero, so no random noise in the system.
	\item The drift remains strictly positive, $(\alpha_1 + 1)dt > 0$.
\end{itemize}

So combining them both, we get:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Xt2}
	dX_{t}^{2}=\left(\alpha _{1}+1-\beta _{1}\frac{X_{t}^{2}}{X_{t}^{1}}\right)dt+\sqrt{2\gamma _{1}\frac{X_{t}^{2}}{X_{t}^{1}}}dW_{t}^{1}
\end{equation}







\medskip






\subsection{\textbf{Inflow Dirtiness $X^3$}}


The equation that determines the dirtiness rate:

\begin{equation}
	dX_t^3 =
\frac{\alpha_2 + 1 - \beta_2 X_t^3}{X_t^1}\,dt
+
\sqrt{
2\gamma_2 \frac{X_t^3}{X_t^1}
}\,dW_t^2
\end{equation}

This equation shows the dirtiness of the incoming sewage $X_t^3$. It assumes the dirtiness tries to return to the normal average level always, and the water tank $X_t^1$ acts as a shield.

\smallskip

The first term of the equation tells us how the dirtiness gets pulled back to the baseline level when it gets very high or low, since:

\begin{equation*}
	\alpha_2 + 1 - \beta_2 X_t^3 = \beta_2\left(\frac{\alpha_2 + 1}{\beta_2} - X_t^3\right)
\end{equation*}

And this is with respect to the tank size ($X_t^1$), because a larger tank size will be able to slow down fast erratic changes better, and dilute the big spikes. 

The second part of the equation explains the randomness of the environmental noise. The square root is so that the dirt level is always possible, since it cant drop below zero. 






\medskip





\subsection{\textbf{Tank 1-2 Pollutant Concentration ($X^4$)}}


Currently, we have the total mass of the stuff inside the tank as $M_t = X_t^1 X_t^4$ (vol x concentration). That gives us,

\begin{equation*}
	dM_t = X_t^1\,dX_t^4 + X_t^4\,dX_t^1
\end{equation*}

Change in mass is the difference between incoming and outgoing mass rate. So,

\begin{equation*}
	dM_t = \left( X_t^2 X_t^3 - X_{\text{out}} X_t^4 \right) dt
\end{equation*}

If we assume that the tank's liquid changes slowly, relative to the concentration changes, we can take $dX_t^1 \approx 0$, and $X_\text{out} \approx X_t^2$. So substituting will get us,

\begin{equation}
	dX_t^4 = (X_t^3 - X_t^4) \frac{X_t^2}{X_t^1}\,dt
\end{equation}


\smallskip

The difference term is that when the incoming sewage is dirtier than the current water, the concentration of the tank increases. If the opposite, the concentration drops. The ratio term is called the \textbf{Turnover Rate}. It is the inverse of hydraulic retention time. When this retention time is long, the ratio will become small, and the tank will act like a smoothen out volatile inflow into a stable stream, $X_4$.




\medskip







\subsection{\textbf{Treated Pollutant Concentration ($X^4$)}}

Conservation of mass gives us,

\begin{equation}
	\frac{dX^5}{dt} = \underbrace{\frac{Q}{V}(X^4 - X^5)}_{\text{Hydraulic dilution}} - \underbrace{R(X^5)}_{\text{Microbial consumption}}
\end{equation}

\smallskip

where V is the reactor volume and Q is the flow.

The Monod Equation for microbial consumption \cite{monodeq} is, 

\begin{equation*}
	R(X^5) = \frac{\mu_{\max} X_{\text{bacteria}}}{Y} \left( \frac{X^5}{K_s + X^5} \right)
\end{equation*}

Using Taylor series we can expand this into,

\begin{align*}
	R(X^5) &\approx R(X^*) + R'(X^*)(X^5 - X^*)\\
	&= \underbrace{( R(X^*) - R'(X^*)X^* )}_{-a} + {R'(X^*)} X^5\\
	&= -a + k X^5
\end{align*}


Let D be the diffusion rate, $D = Q/V$, and some $b \approx D + k$.

\begin{align*}
	\frac{dX^5}{dt} &= D(X^4 - X^5) - ( -a + k X^5 )\\
	&\approx a + b(X^4 - X^5)\\
\end{align*}

\smallskip

And finally,

\begin{equation}
	dX_t^5 = \left( a + b(X_t^4 - X_t^5) \right) dt
\end{equation}






\medskip








\subsection*{\textbf{Set of equations}}

\begin{align*}
	dX_t^1
&=
\left(
\frac{\alpha_1 + 1}{\beta_1} - X_t^2
\right)dt
\\[8pt]
dX_t^2
&=
\left(
\alpha_1 + 1
-
\beta_1 \frac{X_t^2}{X_t^1}
\right)dt
+
\sqrt{
2\gamma_1 \frac{X_t^2}{X_t^1}
}\,dW_t^1
\\[8pt]
dX_t^3
&=
\left(
\frac{\alpha_2 + 1 - \beta_2 X_t^3}{X_t^1}
\right)dt
+
\sqrt{
2\gamma_2 \frac{X_t^3}{X_t^1}
}\,dW_t^2
\\[8pt]
dX_t^4
&=
(X_t^3 - X_t^4)
\frac{X_t^2}{X_t^1}\,dt
\\[8pt]
dX_t^5
&=
\left(
a + b(X_t^4 - X_t^5)
\right)dt
\end{align*}





\medskip




\subsection*{\textbf{Matrix Notation}}


If we write the equations in the following form,

\begin{equation*}
	dX_t = f(X_t) dt + G(X_t) dW_t
\end{equation*}

we can write the set of equations as the sum of vectors in matrices form, such as:

\begin{equation*}
	\begin{bmatrix}
dX^1\\
dX^2\\
dX^3\\
dX^4\\
dX^5
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
f_1(X)\\
f_2(X)\\
f_3(X)\\
f_4(X)\\
f_5(X)
\end{bmatrix}dt
+
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0\\
\sqrt{2\gamma_1\frac{X^2}{X^1}} & 0\\
0 & \sqrt{2\gamma_2\frac{X^3}{X^1}}\\
0 & 0\\
0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
dW^1\\
dW^2
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation*}


\smallskip

We can see that only two out of the five equations have a diffusion term. So we can infer that the randomness exclusively enters through the inflow. The $X^4$ and $X^5$ become stochastic because they contain the terms with diffusion.

\smallskip

This system is called a \textbf{Hypoelliptic System}.







\bigskip








\section{Numerical Solving}

We use Euler-Maruyama method \cite{eulermaruyama} to solve these differential equations.

The interval $[0,T]$ is divided into $N$ equal steps of size,

\begin{equation*}
	\Delta t = \frac{T}{N}
\end{equation*}

and the discrete time being, $t_n = n \Delta t$.

\smallskip

Since Brownian Motion increments follow a normal distribution with mean $0$ and variance $\Delta t$, 

\begin{align*}
	\Delta W &= W_{t + \Delta t} - W_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Delta t)\\
	\Rightarrow \qquad \Delta W_n^k &= W_{(n+1)\Delta t}^k-W_{n\Delta t}^k\\
	&= \sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_n^k
\end{align*}

where $Z_n^k \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$



\medskip


\subsection*{\textbf{Solving the system of DE}}

When applying Euler-Maruyama method to the system, it gives:

\begin{equation}
	X_{n+1}^1 = X_n^1 + \left(  \frac{\alpha+1}{\beta_1} - X_n^2  \right) \Delta t
\end{equation}




\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    X_{n+1}^2
&=
X_n^2+
\left(\alpha_1+1-\beta_1\frac{X_n^2}{X_n^1}\right)\Delta t
\\
&\quad+
\sqrt{2\gamma_1\frac{\widetilde{X}_n^2}{\widetilde{X}_n^1}}
\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_n^1
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}




\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    X_{n+1}^3
&=
X_n^3+
\left(
\frac{\alpha_2+1-\beta_2X_n^3}{X_n^1}
\right)\Delta t
\\
&\quad+
\sqrt{
2\gamma_2\frac{\widetilde{X}_n^3}{\widetilde{X}_n^1}
}
\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_n^2
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}



\begin{align}
	X_{n+1}^4
&=
X_n^4+
(X_n^3-X_n^4)
\frac{X_n^2}{X_n^1}\Delta t
\end{align}




\begin{align}
	X_{n+1}^5
&=
X_n^5+
\left(
a+b(X_n^4-X_n^5)
\right)\Delta t
\end{align}


Since the $X^2$ and $X^3$ terms contain a random variable, if majority of their draws are negative, their values can go to negative. While solving, these negative values can cause imaginary values. So, we set hard boundaries to the values:

\begin{align*}
	\widetilde{X}_n^i&=\max(X_n^i,0)\\
\widetilde{X}_n^1&=\max(X_n^1,\epsilon)
\end{align*}

\smallskip

where $\epsilon = 10^{-4}$. This prevents zero in the denominator as well.
